                                          PREFACE

The Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) is a next generation mini-
facility designed to support Principal Investigators (PI) interested in studying various aspects of
advanced gaseous combustion in a microgravity environment. ACME will be integrated into the
Fluids and Combustion Facility’s (FCF) Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR) onboard the
International Space Station in the US Lab Module.

The ACME mission is to perform microgravity experiments to study fire suppression/flame
extinguishment investigations using the Combustion Integrated Rack. The ACME will allow
investigation using pure fuels and mixtures of fuels at various chamber atmosphere pressures,
compositions, and oxygen concentrations. The microgravity environment allows for
measurement and observation of flame formation and extinguishment processes that cannot be
made in sustained gravity. The modular design of the ACME allows for flexibility in
configuring for specific experiments and easy maintainability of the hardware. This hardware
will provide the apparatus for dispensing, deploying and igniting fuel. When new experiments
are proposed the additional experiment specific hardware will be provided. This requirements
document reflects such a case, including only the new requirements of the ACME investigation.

1.0    INTRODUCTION
1.1    Purpose
This document will define the engineering requirements necessary to successfully complete
science and safety requirements. This document will be used by the ACME design engineers to
develop the systems necessary for ACME. Interface and carrier requirements levied on ACME
will be documented in the ACME ICD, FCF-ICD-CIR-ACME. ACME will use the existing
Multi-User Droplet Combustion Apparatus (MDCA) hardware and software platform design
already developed as a baseline for meeting the requirements found in this document where
applicable. Due to the reuse of the MDCA platform design, ACME operational safety concerns
will be addressed in existing MDCA documents where applicable. Requirements in this
document levied on the ACME carrier, the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR), will be
communicated to the ACME-CIR Interface / Sustaining Engineering Team

1.2    Scope
This Engineering Requirements Document assesses the system requirements and lessons learned
of the existing Multi-user Droplet Combustion Apparatus (MDCA) and Flame Extinguishment
Experiment requirements needed to successfully build the hardware and develop avionics
software and how these requirements are implemented. The Engineering Requirements
Document (ERD) will provide information regarding the ACME experiment and ensure that
requirements are quantifiable, verifiable and specific. The ACME ERD consists of six sets of
requirements: Science Requirements, Operational Requirements, FCF/CIR Integration
Requirements, Derived Requirements, Software Requirements and Safety Requirements.

3.0       REQUIREMENTS MANAGEMENT
3.1       Preparation for Requirements
The preparation for developing proper project requirements is an important first step. Preparing
in this sense means establishing procedures for identifying, approving, allocating and managing
changes to requirements. This document along with the ZIN Requirements Management
Procedure (P04005) is meant to facilitate this process. Other project documentation supports this
effort and their completion is also required. A project shall use the following documents and
procedures during the requirements development process:

     ZIN Requirements Management Procedure P04005
     SpaceDOC II Systems Engineering Management Plan (SEMP) or a Project SEMP as
      necessary T04001
     ZIN Requirements Database T04003
     Project Concept of Operations (ConOps) T04002
     Project Engineering Requirements Document (ERD)

3.1.1 Stakeholder Analysis
An understanding of a customer’s needs and goals for a project is paramount to the requirements
development process. Ultimately, it is the customer who will be using the hardware built to the
specifications laid out by the requirements a project develops. A customer’s satisfaction with the
performance of the developed hardware and the benefits the hardware’s operation provides to
stakeholders is the ultimate measurement of a projects success. Therefore, a project shall
perform a stakeholder analysis in the early phases (Pre-phase A or Phase A) of the project life
cycle.

3.1.1.1       Identify Stakeholders
Project requirements are elicited from Project Stakeholders. A stakeholder is any entity with a
legitimate interest in the project. They are any person or organization that is actively involved in
a project, or whose interests may be positively or negatively affected by execution or completion
of the project. Some stakes an entity may have in your project are: Interest, rights, ownership,
knowledge, impact or influence, contribution, etc. Examples of stakeholders can include:

         ZIN Technologies (further broken into Project Manager, QA Manager, Resource
          Manager, etc.)
         Principal Investigators and Project Scientists
         Customer
         NASA (further broken into GRC, JSC, ISS, etc.)
         Product users
         Product integrator

Stakeholders shall be identified and documented in a Project SEMP using the Project SEMP
template T04001.

3.1.1.2       Identify Level 0 Stakeholder Requirements
To initiate the requirements elicitation process a project should discuss and develop a short list of
the main goals and products of the project. Ideally there would be less than 10 and around 4 or 5
main Level 0 requirements. This step should immediately follow the identification of the Project
Stakeholders.

3.2       Obtaining Requirements
Once the preparations for developing requirements have been made early in the life cycle of a
project, the engineering team can begin to develop a set of requirements that will guide the
design and development of the systems necessary to fulfill the Project Stakeholder’s needs.
These “build-to” or engineering requirements begin to take shape typically in Phase A of a
project life cycle and continue to be developed throughout the design phase of the project.

3.2.1 Identifying All Stakeholder Requirements
After the initial, Level 0, stakeholder requirements have formed a foundation for discussion, a
project can initiate the collection of all stakeholder requirements. Inputs for identifying more
detailed stakeholder requirements will include: Source documents, stakeholder needs and project
constraints such as cost, schedule and hardware/software solution restrictions. Stakeholder
requirements can also be found in interface documents, concept documents developed by the
project, ZIN organizational and industry standards, test results and design maturation
discussions, stakeholder meetings, etc. At this point in the project trade studies are ongoing and
specific hardware and software solutions are beginning to be formulated.

Requirements shall be properly documented with traceability and captured in a Requirements
Development Matrix Database using the ZIN template T04003. Traceability shall include (as
appropriate):

         The source document identity
         The paragraph title
         The sentence number

The requirements will then further be documented using this template as the project’s
Engineering Requirements Document, which is generated from the database.

3.3    Analyzing Requirements
Once all stakeholder requirements have been collected and organized it is essential that these
requirements be analyzed. Ensuring that the requirement statements meet the necessary standard
of quality for communication and implementation is part of this process. Projects shall review
their requirement statements for quality and validity.

3.3.1 Requirement Quality
Requirement statements shall be clear and concise and decomposed to such a level that there is
only one requirement in each requirement statement. Requirements shall use the word “shall” to
clearly identify the demand upon the project team. Some tips to identify poor requirement
statements are:

         Use of superlatives such as best, most
         Subjective language such as “user friendly”, “easy to use”
         Vague pronouns
         Ambiguous adverbs and adjectives such as “almost always”, “significant”, “real-time”,
          “various”, “few”, “many”, “appropriately”
         Open-ended, non-verifiable terms such as “provide support”, “as a minimum”, “but not
          limited to”
         Comparative phrases like “better than” or “higher quality”
         Loopholes such as “if possible”, “as appropriate”
         Other indefinites such as “etc.”, “to be determined (TBD)”

At the time a requirement is identified, a verification method shall also be identified and
documented in the Requirements Database. Verification methods include test, inspection,
demonstration and analysis. This will encourage the writers of the requirement to evaluate
whether they have written a verifiable requirement and provide an instant check of the
requirement’s value. This step shall be done early in the project, typically in Phase B of the
project life cycle.

3.3.2 Requirement Validation
As mentioned above, understanding stakeholders’ needs and goals is an important step in
forming requirements. Ensuring that your requirements are written in a way that fulfills these
expectations is the next step. Along with understanding the manner in which the requirement
will be verified it is also important to analyze whether or not the requirements and the solutions
they offer align themselves with stakeholders initial operational and programmatic goals.

Requirement validation requires forethought as to whether or not the hardware that the
engineering requirements will produce can perform in a manner that fulfills the stakeholders
goals. Simplified, validation becomes the question: Are you building the right hardware? For
example, have your requirements properly captured the precision and accuracy needs of a
particular measurement device? All project requirements shall be validated against stakeholder’s
requirements. This includes any transition or derived requirements.

3.4       Approving Requirements
The approval of the ERD serves as an official method of communication between the
engineering team and the NASA Project Manager. Communication between the engineering
team and NASA stakeholders will have invariably been occurring throughout the requirements
formulation process. However, the ERD approval is where these requirements are formally
agreed upon and baselined.

3.5    Maintaining Requirements
Maintenance of requirements is one of the most essential steps to a project’s requirements
development effort. Even after the ERD has been baselined there could be many instances when
it would need to be updated. A new stakeholder could be identified, maturation of the product
design could bring about new derived requirements from within the engineering team, and of
course frequent review of the requirements could result in finding flaws in the requirement
statements that can be fixed.

Following the logic of this process it is easy to see how many of the project’s fundamental
documents are affected by the requirements. Therefore, when a requirement is modified or
added, each document discussed in this process is also subject to change.

This step shall happen regularly in the Project Life-Cycle to constantly ensure the integrity of the
end product. Requirements documents, the Requirements Development Matrix and associated
project documents shall be updated prior to major project gateway reviews at a minimum.

5.0        ACME HARDWARE PRODUCT DESCRIPTION
The ACME investigation will be using the MDCA hardware and software concepts and design
when appropriate. The ACME hardware consists of three hardware system domains, the
Chamber Insert Assembly (CIA) System, the Avionics Package (AVP) System and the ACME
Imaging Packages (IMAG) System.

The CIA provides the mechanism for the combustion event. The ACME CIA Flow subsystem,
which interfaces with the CIR Fuel/Oxidizer Management Assembly (FOMA), provides gas
mixing and flow control. The AVP is the center of control and power distribution for ACME.
And, the IMAG provides imaging of the combustion event including that provided by the carrier
(CIR).

Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) numbering for traceability across the ACME Project
Lifecycle. The number convention utilizes a SSC (System/Subsystem Classification) followed

5.1    ACME Chamber Insert Assembly (CIA) System
The ACME CIA System provides all the necessary hardware and interfaces to perform science
on one platform. The available interfaces are the CIR Water Thermal Cooling System (WTCS),
and the CIR Fuel/Oxidizer Management Assembly (FOMA). Most experimental functions occur
within the CIA structure and many associated subsystems are found on the CIA. The CIA
consists of the Experiment Mounting Structure (EMS) which is the functional hardware and
upon which the various subsystems are structurally based. Other subsystems found on and
around the CIA include the Gas Flow System (FLOW), Exchangeable Gas Burner System
(Burner), Ignition System (IGN), Engineering Analog Camera System (OpsCam), Diagnostics
System (DIAG), Measurement System (MEAS), E-Field System (E-FIELD).




             FIGURE 3.      ACME Chamber Insert Assembly (CIA) Design

5.2    Experiment Mounting Structure (EMS) System
The EMS provides the main structure for the CIA and acts as the primary mechanical interface
with the CIR combustion chamber. It consists of two endplates, support structure, and extraction
handles.

5.3    Cooling Plate System
ACME will use two cold plates mounted to the Experiment Mounting Structure interfacing with
the CIR Water Thermal Control System (WTCS). One cooling plate can be attached to each end
plate. The hardware to accommodate this is identical to that used on MDCA System. The
cooling plates are plumbed together using rigid tubing, and attached to the IRR WTCS interfaces
with flexible hoses and quick disconnects.

5.4    Gas Flow System (FLOW)
There is a reconfigurable flow system on the CIA that is auxiliary to the CIR FOMA. Gases are
received from FOMA via quick disconnects on the Interface Resource Ring (IRR). The gases
are directed through interchangeable Mass Flow Controllers, which are replaced as experiments
require. Gases are then directed through a series of switching and isolation valves to the burner
or directly vented into the chamber for chamber fills.

5.5    Gas Burner System (Burner)
The burner system consists of three different types of exchangeable burners made from varying
inner and outer diameter stainless steel tubing supported by an electrically insulating ceramic
base. There are 4 types of burners: Spherical (3), Gas Jet (7), Co-flow (1) and Burning Rate
Emulator (2). Each burner tip is smooth and without burs or sharp edges to aid in the gas
deployment. The burners are mounted with a coupling that allows exchangeability of the burners
and maintains the precise alignment of the system. Spherical and Gas-Jet burners also include
burner temperature thermocouples.

5.6    Ignition System (IGN)
The Ignition System will reuse the design of the MDCA System igniter, with a different
deployment scheme.

The ignition system consists of a hot wire igniter subassembly mounted to a deployment arm. A
Haydon-Kerk linear actuator motor and driver board controls the position of the deployment arm.
The igniter tip is mounted to an insulating ceramic structure attached to the end of the arm. The
entire top assembly is removed from the arm and replaced on an experiment basis to maintain the
precise geometry necessary for igniter functionality and to reposition the igniter tip based on the
burner tip’s position.

5.7    Analog Camera System (OpsCam)
The Analog Camera System will be provided, internal to the combustion chamber, primarily as a
near real-time visual aid to engineering for hardware operation monitoring and troubleshooting.
In addition, this view will provide a near real-time “look” into the chamber for science
throughout the flame combustion event.

5.8    Illumination System (OpsCam)
The Illumination System consists of an array of high intensity, white light LEDs that receive
power from the relay board hardware and are controlled by the Data Acquisition Unit. The

illumination system intensity will be variable to avoid saturation of diagnostics. This
illumination system is provided to support the Analog Camera System.

5.9    Diagnostic System (DIAG)
Two of the diagnostic systems the Radiometers, the Photomultiplier Tube Modules (PMTs), and
the Thin Fiber Pyrometry (TFP) Array are located on the Insert. This hardware supports
Diagnostic Analysis of the Flames.

5.10   Radiometer System
The Radiometers will provide relative measure of flame radiation and emissions, over the
wavelength range of 0.2 m – 11 m, throughout the combustion event. The Radiometer System
will be mounted on the CIA providing a direct view of the combustion event. Data from the
Radiometers will be handled by the Data Acquisition Unit mounted inside the Chamber. Power
via the Relay Board to the Radiometer System is provided by ACME AVP System (section
3.4.2).

5.11   Photomultiplier Tube (PMT) System
The Photomultiplier Tube Package will provide high-sensitivity relative measurements of flame
radiation and chemiluminescent emissions of electromagnetic energy over a wide range of
frequencies and intensity levels throughout the combustion event as well as flame detection and
extinction determination. The PMT System consists of three Hamamatsu PMT modules,
mounted on the CIA providing a direct view of the combustion event. Additional filters will be
required for the 2 PMT modules used for narrowband emissions. Data from the PMT modules
will be handled by the Data Acquisition Unit mounted inside the Chamber. Power via the Relay
Board to the PMT System is provided by ACME AVP System (section 3.4.2).

5.12   Thin Fiber Pyrometry (TFP) Array System
A Thin Fiber Pyrometry (TFP) Array arm mounted to a translating motor to move the array into
and out of the flame. The translating motor will be an Ultramotion brand linear stepper motor
and encoder, similar to that used by MDCA System for its igniter. The Array will be stowed out
of the combustion area during E-Field testing, or when the Array is not required, but will not be
removed from the chamber. The array can be aligned with either the HiBMs-2 or ACME data
color camera.

5.13   Measurement System (MEAS)
The ACME measurement system encompasses the measurement subsystems involved in
detection and measurement of the experiments with the exception of diagnostic systems
mentioned above in section 3.4.1.6 and CIR provided diagnostics. The following sensors are a
part of the Measurement System: Far Field Thermocouple Array, Pressure Transducers for
differential pressure measurement, and heat flux sensors for the BRE burners.

5.14   E-Field System (E-FIELD)
The E-Field system consists of two High Voltage (HV) sources (one negative and one positive
polarity), a passive network, an insulated mesh electrode and an insulated burner electrically
connected to the returns of the HV supplies.

5.16   Avionics Package (AVP)
The Avionics Package will house the main electronics and data storage for the ACME hardware

A main computer processor is responsible for controlling CIA hardware, imaging hardware to
execute test points as well as acquiring, storing, and downlinking experiment data. Experiment
control and sequencing will be uplinked from the ACME Ground System (AGS) software, at the
TSC, to the ACME Main Processor (AMP) System software that is resident on the main

5.16.2 Color Camera Data Store (CDS) System
The ACME Color Camera data is stored on a hard drive in the AVP. The hard drive is
removable and replaceable on orbit.

5.16.3 Power Distribution System (PDS)
The AVP Power & Distribution System provides power to the equipment in within the AVP and
the CIA via four 28VDC, 8 amp circuits from the CIR’s Electrical Power Control Unit (EPCU).

custom designed relay boards. These devices handle all of the communications with the AVP as
well as the activation of various components of the CIA.

5.17   ACME Imaging Package System (IMAG)
The ACME Imaging (IMAG) System provides all optical imaging and non-optical imaging

UML Location on the Optics Bench. Power to the Data Color Camera Package is supplied via
the UML location.

ACME will utilize the CIR LLL-UV, HiBMS-1, and HiBMS-2 Packages.

7.0    ACME ENGINEERING REQUIREMENTS
Engineering requirements are ACME’s build-to requirement response to science requirements
levied by NASA. This section is drawn from the information filled into the ZIN Requirements
Development Matrix (T04003) using the Requirements Management Procedure (P04005.)
ACME’s requirements are organized according to the project’s PBS. This helps to keep the
reader and reviewer thinking in terms of the whole system.

The SSC_ID column is for useful organization and sorting of the system requirements in the
Requirements Tracking Matrix (RTM) into categories by system, subsystem, or other traits for
measurements and metrics that may be of interest. The SSC (System/Subsystem Classification)
is a three digit number which relates to the Product Breakdown Structure (PBS) which in turn is
a subset of the Work Breakdown Structure (WBS). The first digit of the SSC relates the
requirement to the system assignment. The second digit of the SSC relates the requirement to
the subsystem assignment. The third digit of the SSC is used to relate the requirement to the
product classification or type: flight hardware product (0), flight software product (1), or ground
software product (2), in which they affect. The SSC is followed by an underscore as a delineator
and then a unique four digit identification number. The underscore, versus the dash, is used to
keep the SSC and ID married as one object in the database. Requirements with like qualities of
interest may be grouped together by ID number for ease of sorting.
