1. Introduction
The narrative description of the experimental procedure is an excerpt from the ‘Methods / Experimental’
section of the following conference paper.
    Y.-C. Chien, J. Tinajero, D. Stocker, U. Hegde, and D. Dunn-Rankin, “Electric Field Effects on Flames in
    Microgravity on the International Space Station,” 2018 Spring Technical Meeting of the Central States
    Section of The Combustion Institute, Minneapolis, MN, 20-22 May 2018.

While the paper was written for the Electric-Field Effects on Laminar Diffusion Flames (E-FIELD Flames)
experiment, much of the discussion applies to ACME in general, including the s-Flame investigation. The
figure referenced in this excerpt is not included herein. Clarifications and additional information, not in
the conference paper, are included in boxed notes like this.

The CIR experiments are configured, but not conducted, by the ISS crew. Thus far, ACME’s activity has
included the majority of the crew members, regardless of their nationalities. Most often the astronauts
and cosmonauts exchange gas bottles, mass flow controllers, and igniter tips. In the latter case, the igniter
tip exchange can occur after failure or proactively before failure when the crew already has changes to
make changes in the interior of the insert. While these activities may seem mundane, the crew play a
critical role in setting up the hardware for each experiment. As an extreme example, there were crew
activities on 8 separate days in Sept.-Oct. 2017 to configure the CIR and ACME hardware for the first ACME
experiment, namely the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment. In contrast, only two
crew days were required to reconfigure the hardware for the first E-FIELD Flames tests because of the
initial use of the same coflow burner. Planning is critical because crew activity is scheduled three weeks
in advance, for which detailed procedures must be prepared and uplinked to the ISS.

Similarly, the s-Flame experiment was conducted using the same burner as the Flame Design investigation
which preceded it. However, there was hardware reconfiguration for s-Flame to enable use of an
additional intensified camera for imaging OH* chemiluminescence, namely the Low Light Level Ultraviolet
(LLL-UV) camera.

All CIR tests are remotely commanded from the ground from NASA’s Glenn ISS Payload Operations Center
(GIPOC) in Cleveland, Ohio. Typically, four to six people staff four consoles during the testing. The CIR
hardware is controlled from the Rack Officer (RO) and Data Management Officer (DMO) consoles, and the
ACME hardware is controlled from the Payload Developer (PD) console. Two shifts of personnel are
scheduled to staff these three consoles on test days because they typically last 14 hours with the many
pre- and post-testing activities. Given that the tests are remotely commanded, the operations are carried
out in the day and evening in Cleveland, e.g., from 7 AM to 9 PM. A day’s tests, i.e., with flames, are
normally conducted in a period of 10 hours of less. Tests are normally conducted on three weekdays per
two weeks. On intervening weekdays, the image data is downlinked and the crew often swap one or both
gas bottles. Transferring and downlinking data from a day’s tests also requires a 2-shift day for the RO
and DMO consoles, where it is always necessary to verify that data was downlinked without errors and
that data is not missing. Those two positions are also staffed to guide and oversee the crew activity. As
a result, the CIR consoles are normally actively staffed on four weekdays per week. A team of four carry
out the detailed planning for the CIR and ACME console staff, and the daily operational plans typically
exceed 50 pages on days including testing.

The science console allows for review of the analog video and non-imaging telemetry (at 1 Hz) in near real
time but doesn’t allow for commanding. In the case of E-FIELD Flames, arrangements were made so that

the analog video is streamed to a secure computer at UC Irvine to allow the research team to participate
in test operations and to recommend adjustments to the day’s planned parameters. That team does not
see the telemetry during the testing, but is in communication via phone and e-mail with their NASA
research partners at the GRC ISS Payload Operations Center (GIPOC). While there are many preparatory
steps, the tests are ultimately conducted using an uploaded software script and parameter files. In
advance of the test day, the UC Irvine science team plans the nearly 60 parameters in the voltage sweep
script and specifies other parameters that are configured outside of the script, such as camera settings.
It is also possible to effectively conduct tests in a ‘manual’ mode, where parameters are changed in
response to the analog video downlink or telemetry. For example, the electric field potential can be
adjusted to approach a stability or soot limit.

Provisions have been made to enable the investigators from all ACME experiments to remotely view the
analog video while they communicated verbally with NASA science personnel in the operations center.
Thus far, all ACME flame tests, including those for s-Flame and E-FIELD Flames, have been conducted in an
automated manner using software scripts and extensive parameter files.

Typically, ten E-FIELD Flames tests are conducted per test day. Despite the short test durations, the low
rate results from several unavoidable factors. After a test is conducted, the 125-Hz non-imaging data is
downlinked for immediate review by the science team. A small sample of images are often downlinked
to ensure that the camera exposures are appropriate, where the times for those images are typically
selected based on non-imaging data to provide extreme examples. The review helps to verify parameters
or plan for adjustments in subsequent tests, e.g., to select the voltage range after determining where the
ion current saturates for the given flame conditions. After a test is conducted, a fan circulates the chamber
atmosphere after which the chamber is vented (to space) back to atmospheric pressure, if needed.
Midway through a day’s E-FIELD Flames tests, the atmosphere is circulated for 15 minutes through a filter
cartridge filled with molecular sieves to capture any soot and adsorb at least a portion of the combustion
products, e.g., carbon dioxide, water vapor, and incomplete combustion products. The tests are only
conducted when both Ku and S band communication is available through NASA’s Tracking and Data Relay
Satellite (TDRS) System. Depending on the various orbits, there are periodic communication outages with
a loss of signal (LOS) that can be a short handover (from one satellite to another) or can last as long as 30
minutes.

In contrast to E-FIELD Flames, an average of 15 flames were ignited per test day for s-Flame, but they were
of even shorter durations. The pressure rise with an s-Flame test was small and the chamber was rarely
vented after a test unless the chamber atmosphere was being adjusted for other purposes. In this regard,
the chamber atmosphere was sometimes scrubbed for the s-Flame investigation by recirculating it through
the adsorber cartridge, but this was not a regular routine as in the case of E-FIELD Flames. The chamber
was also sometimes partially or completely vented and refilled during s-Flame operations, for example to
adjust the atmosphere’s oxygen concentration.

Unlike E-FIELD Flames, the s-Flame experiment also made use of the CIR’s Gas Chromatograph
Instrumentational Package (GCIP) to measure species concentrations of the atmosphere in the combustion
chamber. There is no sampling probe and measurements are only made before, between, or after tests
when the chamber atmosphere was ‘stirred’ through use of a fan. For s-Flame, the GC measurements
were made on most but not all test days, but they were limited in scope because of the significant time
required. The GC measurements were sometimes only made at the end of the test day but were
occasionally made earlier in the day, for example prior to an adjustment of the chamber atmosphere.

A variety of constraints limit the number of tests that can be performed, both overall and on a daily basis.
The next combustion experiments in the CIR will feature solid fuels and focus on flame spread and material
flammability. That follow-on plan limits the duration of ACME operations, where each ACME experiment
is allotted four months for testing. Although it requires additional crew time, these allotments are split
into two rounds so that the investigators have a break from testing for careful review of the early results
so that they can maximize the scientific outcome of their second round. This paper reports on early results
from the first round of E-FIELD Flames.

In contrast to earlier plans, the two rounds of the s-Flame experiment occurred sequentially because of
limited crew availability. Due to delays in commercial crew transport, the space station had three rather
six crew members through much of 2020. As a result of the reduced crew size, the s-Flame investigators
did get a 3-month break (in Feb. to April) between their two rounds of flame tests allowing them to study
early results and prepare for the second round. Hardware issues also stretched out s-Flame operations in
the latter half of 2019.

While nitrogen is available from the space station, the combustion testing is limited by the available gas
bottles that were and are launched to support the tests. For E-FIELD Flames and most of ACME’s
experiments, the test matrices used to plan those gas resources were established in 2010. At that time,
NASA limited the scope of the ACME tests matrices with the intent of focused experiments with limited
objectives rather than comprehensive experiments. As such, the general target was 50 tests per
experiment. For most ACME experiments, the limiting gas constraint is the oxygen/nitrogen bottles used
to fill the chamber. This resource constraint is exacerbated by the CIR because it is equipped with a pair
of redundant safety timers to limit the fuel that can flow into the combustion chamber (where the timers
can be set for the oxidizer flow in the case of inverse flame testing). The timers can only be reset by
venting the combustion chamber. Therefore, the timer limit combined with other constraints effectively
corresponds to the day’s maximum flow time for the fuel (or more generally, the limiting reactant). For
E-FIELD Flames, the safety timers are set to the maximum value of 999 seconds, which corresponds to the
10 tests typically completed per day.

The second round of the s-Flame experiment featured helium dilution of both the fuel and the oxidizer, so
its testing was also limited by the oxygen/helium bottles launched for that purpose.

The 999-second limit on the valve safety timers has thus far been common for all ACME testing. However,
the timers can be reset to 999 by nearly completely venting the chamber. Sufficient time and gas resources
are needed to vent and refill the chamber and still conduct tests afterward and that was only occasionally
done for s-Flame.

Another constraint is the available time in a day, where the console personnel and ISS resources, such as
the video downlink, are planned for a limited number of hours. When necessary for the research, NASA
does have continuous 24-hour/day operations for some non-CIR experiments. But as discussed, the ACME
tests are typically two minutes or less in duration, so the testing is almost always limited to the planned
durations.

Data limits are yet another constraint, where the on-orbit hardware can store a limited amount of data.
More importantly, 127,000 MB is the most that can be reasonably managed in a two-shift transfer and
downlink day. Therefore, tests days are limited to this amount of data, where image data from select

2. Test Sequence and Parameters
All s-Flames tests conducted on the ISS were carried out using automated software which had its roots in
ACME’s Integrated Science Requirements Documents (ISRD, RDR version, Rev. B) and Science Operations
Requirements Documents (SORD, Rev. A). The s-Flame Science Requirements Document (SRD) is of course
pertinent but only that which was incorporated in the ISRD and SORD was used by the engineering team
in developing the control software.

The major phases of the test sequence are graphically displayed in Figures 1 and 2, which are respectively
for the two-rounds of s-Flame testing. The first round was carried out with nitrogen dilution of the fuel
and atmosphere, where that inert gas was available from the space station. Meanwhile, the second round
was carried out with fuel diluted in advance (on the ground) with helium. Similarly, bottles of 30% oxygen
with a balance of helium (on a volume basis) were launched for this testing, where that was often diluted
with 100% helium (also launched for s-Flame) to conduct tests in a 21% oxygen atmosphere.

The lines appearing in the Figures 1 and 2 represent the inert (i.e., nitrogen or helium) flow rate, the fuel
flow rate, the igniter position and power. The lines that are more lightly shaded represent some optional
changes to flow rates, for example for a change in flow rate(s) following ignition. However, the flow
variations in the darkly shaded lines are also optional. The phases are described in detail at the bottom
of each schematic, where the nomenclature throughout these figures generally corresponds to the script
parameter names.

3. s-Flame Script

tests is presented here. This is the final version, used in the second round of testing, which incorporates
a few minor changes that were made during operations:
         2020-01-14      Added ignition detection prior to igniter arm retraction
         2020-03-11      burnerOverLimitSec variable changed to global scope
         2020-03-13      Fixed ramp-holds not ramping to desired flow delta
The first change was implemented near the end of the first round of testing, while the other two changes
were made between the two rounds.

function checkExtinctionDetected(flowStartTime, timeoutSec, abortTest)
{
  // if abortTest is true this throws a message if extinction is detected
  // or if the timeout has been exceeded
  // if abortTest is false this returns 2 if extinction is detected, 0 if extinction
  // is disabled, 3 if a timeout has been exceeded, and 1 if none of these is true.
  var currentTime = new Date().getTime();

    var timedOut = false;
    var detected = false;
    var disabled = false;
    if((currentTime - flowStartTime) >= (timeoutSec * 1000))
    {
       timedOut = true;
    throw "Flow timeout reached";
    }

    if ( ExtinctionDetectionTelemetry.extinctionDetectionState == 2)

                                                      34
ACME/s-Flame Experimental Procedure                                            8 April 2021


    {
        detected = true;
    }

    if ( ExtinctionDetectionTelemetry.extinctionDetectionState == 0)
    {
        disabled = true;
    }

    if (abortTest==true)
    {
        if (detected==true)
        { throw "EXTINCTION DETECTED"; }
    }

    if ( detected == true)
    { return 2;}
    else if ( timedOut == true )
    { return 3;}
    else if ( disabled == true )
    { return 0; }
    else
    { return 1; }

}


function updateDurationRemaining(startTime, startDurationSecs)
{

  SystemTelemetry.scriptDurationRemaining = startDurationSecs - elapsedSecs;
  return SystemTelemetry.scriptDurationRemaining;
}

4. Test Matrices
For all ACME experiments, including s-Flame, the test planning is refined in multiple steps as described in
this section. Despite refinement, the experiments are always expected to address the science objectives
approved in the ACME ISRD.

     A. Test Matrices: Years in Advance
           The test matrices found in section 5 of the ACME Integrated Science Requirements Document
           (ISRD, RDR version, Rev. B) were the basis for planning the equipment and resources, such as gas
           bottles, needed to carry out each of the NASA-selected ACME experiments. For s-Flame, the test
           matrices are found in section 5.4. The investigators were each directed to define matrices with
           required testing which included at most one burner configuration and no more than nominally 50
           flames ignited. Regarding these constraints specified by Project Scientist Dennis Stocker, the
           intent was to conduct focused rather than comprehensive research given the considerable
           resources required for testing on the International Space Station. Meanwhile, there were no
           limits put on desired or highly desired tests because of NASA’s plan to only carry out tests
           designated as required. However, in the case of s-Flame, the decision was made to also carry out
           their highly desired tests, i.e., treating them as required, so that its testing would be comparable
           to that of the other NASA-selected ACME experiments.


                                                       36
ACME/s-Flame Experimental Procedure                                                            8 April 2021


       NASA Glenn’s ISS Control Board later decided to limit ACME testing to nominally 4 months of ISS
       testing per experiment which with preparatory operations, minor delays, repeat testing due to
       science loss, etc. were expected to typically occur in a total of 5 months. This decision was made
       with the expectation that the required testing, e.g., as specified in the ISRD, could at least mostly
       be accomplished in that time period assuming the availability of crew and other needed
       resources.

       As part of his planning, Stocker instigated an alternating sequence of experiment testing, wherein
       investigators would get a break in their testing in order to analyze early results in depth and with
       that knowledge achieve higher science outcomes in their second round of testing. As a result,
       each experiment was expected to get 2 months of testing per round, which meant nominally 8
       test days per round of testing. Within that time constraint and available resources, experiments
       were ultimately permitted to plan for and conduct more tests than had been specified as required
       in the ISRD, despite the initial planning which ignored desired and highly desired testing.

   B. Test Matrices: Months in Advance
       At least a few months in advance of testing, ACME investigators are directed to submit a refined
       test matrix for the upcoming round corresponding to available hardware, gas bottles, and the
       agreed number of test days. The refinement can be based on what is learned from experimental,
       analytical, or computational efforts subsequent to the 2013 ISRD. Generally, this planning has
       been done in detail comparable to that found in the ISRD, with the important exception that it
       must furthermore establish the required hardware, including the specific mass flow controllers
       (and thus specification of the flow ranges) and gas bottles so that overall experiment operations,
       including crew activity, can be adequately planned. In this regard, it must establish general
       operational plans for the chamber atmosphere on each day of testing including filling, scrubbing
       (via circulation through the adsorber cartridge), venting (whether partial or complete), and
       measurements, if any, with the Gas Chromatograph Instrumentation Package (GCIP).

   C. Test Matrices: Days in Advance
       The investigators are required to submit their test parameters for all planned tests by noon
       Eastern at least three business days in advance of each scheduled test day. That submission
       includes all of the test parameters within the script and additional ones which are outside of the
       script Most of the latter deal with the camera settings which don’t change during a test, for
       example the filters used with the ACME Data Camera (DataCam) and the Cool Flames Investigation
       (CFI) intensified camera. Outside of the script, the investigators must again specify the chamber
       atmosphere(s) and when GCIP measurements, if any, are planned where those must correspond
       to the plan submitted months earlier. The script parameters, e.g., specifications of flow rates,
       don’t need to correspond to those previously specified, but they must correspond to the
       hardware and gas bottles planned months earlier and generally the chamber atmospheres as well
       by extension.

   D. Test Matrices: As-Run
       Within constraints, the ACME tests conducted can use different parameter values than those
       specified in advance. While the planning is critically important, the plans themselves are not
       particularly useful for interpreting the data. This is because most tests were conducted with
       parameter values that differed in at least a few ways from those planned days earlier.

       While it may seem obvious and simple enough to focus attention on the as-run parameter values
       (where an example s-Flame file name is as run20176A1.txt), that is unfortunately not the case for
       ACME because the as-run values were not always completely recorded, despite the
       implementation of automated recording. The most assured way to get the as-run parameter
       values is presumably from the test day’s automated ACME log file, where an example name is
       2020-06-24_15-10_ACME.log.
