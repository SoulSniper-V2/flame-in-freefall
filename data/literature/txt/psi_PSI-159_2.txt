1. Introduction
The purpose of this report is to provide a description of the experimental hardware and operations of
used in the Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) project that is sufficient for
informed interpretation of the experimental results. In contrast, it is not the intent of this document to
review the project management, engineering development, design process, etc. although such aspects
may be briefly addressed.

The ACME suite of experiments are being carried out on the International Space Station (ISS) in the
Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR). ACME features investigations with gaseous fuels, such as methane and
ethylene. In addition to the fuel state, the current ACME experiments use laminar, non-premixed (i.e.,
diffusion) flames for their studies. To simplify analysis, these flames are furthermore either axisymmetric
or spherically symmetric, depending upon the experiment.

Currently, ACME includes six independent experiments of which the first five were selected for testing in
spaceflight by NASA while the sixth was selected by the Center for the Advancement of Science in Space
(CASIS) and the National Science Foundation (NSF).

The CIR facility was launched in 2008 and with the Multi-user Droplet Combustion Apparatus (MDCA) was
used for a series of liquid-fueled experiments from 2009 to 2017. The facility was reconfigured and set
up for ACME testing by the ISS crew in September and October 2017, where over 25 astronauts from
Canada, Germany, Italy, Japan, Russia and the United States have thus far supported the project’s
research. As of this writing, over one thousand ACME flames have been ignited on the ISS, where that
began in Nov. 2017. It is expected that the planned ACME testing will be completed in 2021, when the
CIR operations should subsequently shift to the Solid Fuel Ignition and Extinction (SoFIE) project.

The CIR facility was preceded by the Combustion Module (CM) which flew on the space shuttle Columbia.
In 1997, the combustion research facility was within Spacelab on the STS-83 and STS-94 (Microgravity
Science Laboratory) missions. It was subsequently reconfigured and flew in 2003 within SPACEHAB, again in
the shuttle’s payload bay, in the STS-107 mission. The Combustion Module investigations included Laminar
Soot Processes (LSP), Structure Of Flame Balls At Low Lewis-number (SOFBALL), and the Water-Mist Fire
Suppression Experiment (Mist). Although all three experiments burned gaseous fuel, each had its own
chamber insert, whereas ACME features a single modular chamber insert to accomplish multiple
investigations. A significant difference between the CIR and its shuttle precursor is that the ISS tests are
remotely commanded from the ground without crew involvement, which wasn’t the case with the
Combustion Module research. But in all cases the crew are required to configure the necessary hardware.

2. Engineering Overview
   A. General Description
       This section provides a general description of the ACME engineering from the ‘3.0 Experimental’
       section of the following 2014 conference paper:
               Dennis P. Stocker, Fumiaki Takahashi, J. Mark Hickman, and Andrew C. Suttles, “Gaseous
               Non-Premixed Flame Research Planned for the International Space Station,” Spring
               Technical Meeting of the Central States Section of the Combustion Institute, Tulsa, OK, 16–
               18 March 2014.

       IMPORTANT: The 2104 paper from which this section was extracted proceeded completion of the
       spaceflight hardware which was mostly launched in 2017. As a result, it does not accurately
       describe the final engineering in some regards. Therefore updates, clarifications, additional
       details, shortcomings, references, etc. have been included in boxed notes like this following the
       original description. As a minor note, the sizes of figures were adjusted in this report, but none
       were cropped.

       As previously discussed, the ACME experiments will be conducted within the Combustion
       Integrated Rack (CIR) using a chamber insert, depicted in Figure 2, which was designed to
       accommodate gas delivery to a variety of exchangeable burners. Besides the chamber insert,
       other ACME-unique hardware includes a high-definition color camera and an avionics package,
       which is for data acquisition and control.




         Figure 2: Design of the Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) chamber
                    insert that will be used in the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR) on ISS.

       ACME’s chamber insert, which is ~0.4 m in diameter, provides common mounting for a variety of
       burners on the chamber axis (but only one at a time) and includes a retracting resistive igniter. As
       depicted in Figure 3, there will initially be gas-jet burners (with inner diameters ranging from 0.4
       to 3.2 mm), a small coflow burner (where the two concentric tubes have inner diameters of 2.1
       and 25 mm), porous spherical burners (with diameters ranging from 6.35 to 12.7 mm), and two
       flat porous burners for the BRE experiment (with inner diameters of 25 and 50 mm). Most burners
       will be equipped with a surface thermocouple, and each BRE burner will have two heat flux
       transducers. The internal pressure of the porous burners will be measured to allow monitoring
       of the pressure differential across the outlet surface. The igniter will have a crew-exchangeable
       tip and arm (to accommodate the various burners) and its stepper-motor drive will allow for
       precise positioning.




          Figure 3: ACME burner types, including from left to right, gas-jet burners, a co-flow burner,
                  porous spherical burners, and flat porous burners for the BRE experiment.

       Ultimately, the spherical burners were limited to a single diameter of 6.35 mm, and the fabricated
       gas-jet burners were limited to two sizes with nominal inner diameters of 1.3 and 2.1 mm.
       However, only the larger gas-jet burner was used during the spaceflight tests. The burners are
       addressed in detail in section 3.C.

       Unfortunately, the spherical burners have not always yielded spherically symmetric flames,
       presumably as a result of (1) greater burner flow through the joint between the porous sphere and
       supply tube, and (2) heat loss to the support tube (encompassing the supply tube) causing local
       flame quenching.

       Two types of igniter tips have been used during ACME testing. Most tests have been conducted
       using a loop igniter tip like those which had been successfully used in the droplet combustion
       experiments which preceded ACME research in the CIR. However, some tests were conducted with
       a coil igniter tip, where that included the second round testing for both the Burning Rate Emulator
       (BRE) and Structure and Response of Spherical Diffusion Flames (s-Flame) experiments, with the
       exception of the latter’s first day testing. The CFI-G investigation will also use the coil igniter tip.
       For more information about the igniter tips, see section 3.D.

       The chamber insert includes three mass flow controllers (visible on the left of Fig. 2) for the
       delivery of fuel, oxidizer or inert (e.g., helium or carbon dioxide), and nitrogen to the burner. The
       maximum fuel flow is 2 slpm (on a nitrogen basis), whereas much higher flows, e.g., 10 slpm (on
       a nitrogen basis), are possible for the oxidizer and nitrogen lines. The crew can exchange the mass
       flow controllers to provide appropriate ranges that maximize the flow accuracy. The gas delivery
       system enables on-orbit dilution of the fuel or oxidizer (but not both simultaneously), coflow,

                                                     6
ACME Engineering Implementation                                                                19 May 2022


       inverse flames (e.g., oxidizer delivery to the center of a coflow burner), and premixed flames,
       although the latter are not planned for any of the current ACME experiments. Gases can also be
       delivered premixed to the space station where that is necessary in some circumstances, such as
       the blending of different fuel gases. CIR’s standard bottle sizes are 1, 2.25, and 3.8 liters but ACME
       will sometimes use smaller bottles as a hazard control to limit the deficient reactant (e.g., fuel)
       that can be released into the combustion chamber.

       Thus far, ACME tests were not conducted with carbon dioxide as a diluent. Furthermore, the fuel
       diluted with helium was premixed prior to launch rather than on orbit. Ultimately, oxidizer flows
       of up to 20 slpm (on a nitrogen basis) were possible, while the nitrogen flow was limited to a
       maximum of 5 slpm. ACME has not used gas bottles smaller than 1 liter as indicated above; the
       bottle fill pressure was instead limited as an additional hazard control.

       ACME’s primary flame diagnostics are a suite of imaging systems including five video cameras and
       two illumination systems, of which three cameras are part of the CIR facility and are already in
       use on orbit. All but one of the cameras is mounted outside of the chamber. Collectively, the
       imaging systems will enable color, CH*, and OH* imaging; pyrometry; measurement of the soot
       volume fraction; and operational support. However, it is unlikely that all functions would be
       exercised at any one time as will become apparent in the subsequent discussion.

       Color imaging is provided by two cameras: a high-definition digital camera mounted outside of
       the chamber and a standard analog camera mounted on the chamber insert (with a turning
       mirror). Both cameras are part of the ACME hardware that is now in development. The analog
       camera signal will be downlinked in near real time to support the experiment operations. Light-
       emitting diodes within the chamber can be used to illuminate the igniter and burner to both
       support the operations and provide reference images. The 12-bit digital camera records at up to
       30 full frames (1360x1024) per second, and is equipped with a motorized lens with independent
       control of the zoom, iris, and focus. The digital camera will be equipped with a filter assembly
       with four options, where selection of a clear “filter” allows for color imaging. Color imaging can
       also be carried out with a blue-green BG7 Schott glass filter, which balances the color planes to
       avoid saturation in the red while maintaining sensitivity in the blue and green. The natural flame
       color can be approximately reconstructed, but the primary purpose of the filter is pyrometry and
       measurement of the soot volume fraction, again via the techniques of [23-24]. Alternately, CH*
       imaging can be conducted with this camera because the filter assembly includes a 430-nm
       bandpass filter with a 10-nm full width at half-maximum transmission (FWHM). A 450-nm
       bandpass filter (again with a 10-nm FWHM) can be used with the CH* filter to effectively subtract
       interfering broadband soot emission. However, the filter assembly rotates slowly so such
       subtraction effectively requires a separate test with each filter and of course reproducible flame
       conditions.

       The video from the analog operations (i.e., ops) camera, also referred to as the internal camera,
       is not archived in space. The video is instead recorded on the ground in the operations center. Its
       gain was sometimes adjusted for improved imaging of dim flames, e.g., approaching extinction.

       The sensitivity of the color digital camera, often referenced as the ACME DataCam (i.e., Data
       Camera), to the dim microgravity flames was such that it was often used with long exposure times
       and thus low framing rates, for example on the order of 1 frame per second. The clear “filter” is

ACME Engineering Implementation                                                               19 May 2022


       of fused silica. Unfortunately, the thickness of the blue-green BG7 Schott glass filter and the added
       antireflective (AR) coating caused too great a reduction in the red, so it does not balance the
       intensity of the three color planes as intended and thereby can limit the accuracy of temperature
       determination. Meanwhile, rather than the 450-nm bandpass filter described above, the high
       definition color camera was equipped with a bandpass filter for imaging formaldehyde emissions.
       For more information, see the Data Camera Characterization Report (ACME-RPT-010 Rev. A).

       Imaging of the dim hydrogen flames in the Structure and Response of Spherical Diffusion Flames
       (s-Flame) experiment was particularly challenging with these, if not all, cameras.

       Pyrometry can alternately be accomplished with CIR’s monochrome High-Bit Depth Multispectral
       (HiBMs) camera, equipped with a Liquid-Crystal Tunable Filter (LCTF), which allows for imaging at
       multiple spectra, but requires a quasi-steady flame for measurement at each desired band. This
       camera has a telecentric optical system and is recorded at 512x512 and 30 frames per second
       (fps). Although it has a 1024x1024 pixel array, binning has been necessary because of signal loss
       through the LCTF. Both soot pyrometry and Thin Filament Pyrometry (TFP) are possible, where
       ACME is equipped with a crew-replaceable array of 5 silicon carbide (SiC) fibers. The fibers are
       orthogonal to the flame axis and the array can be translated along the flame axis to the desired
       position downstream of the burner, e.g., based on the downlink of the analog video. During set
       up, the crew can manually orient the array so that it is orthogonal to either ACME’s color digital
       camera or the HiBMs camera with the LCTF. An alternate fiber array including a platinum wire
       will be used for inflight characterization, which in combination with preflight calibration will
       enable determination of the soot volume fraction [24].

       In contrast to the preceding paragraph, ACME did not use a HiBMs camera for pyrometry, but
       instead relied on use of ACME’s color DataCam for such temperature measurements. For an
       example of how this can be done, see the techniques described in the following papers:
       P.B. Kuhn, B. Ma, B.C. Connelly, M.D. Smooke, and M.B. Long, “Soot and Thin-filament Pyrometry
           Using a Color Digital Camera,” Proceedings of the Combustion Institute, 33 (2011) 743-750.
       B. Ma, M. B. Long, “Absolute light calibration using S-type thermocouples,” Proceedings of the
           Combustion Institute, 34 (2) (2013) 3531-3539.

       Thus far, TFP tests have been conducted with 14-micron fibers spaced apart by 5 mm for a total
       span of 20 mm (in the case of 5 intact ceramic fibers), neglecting optional translation of the array.
       Tests have been conducted using arrays with and without the platinum wire in the furthest
       downstream position. Tests have also been conducted with some of the fibers broken and
       subsequently removed, leaving only three fibers in the final testing for the BRE and Coflow Laminar
       Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiments and at least the initial testing for the Cool Flames
       Investigation with Gases (CFI-G).

       CIR has a second HiBMs camera, without a filter, that will be used in combination with CIR’s
       illumination package for alternate measurements of the soot volume fraction. This will be
       accomplished via an extinction technique previously employed in microgravity [3,6,26], where the
       camera and the collimated light source (with its two fiber-coupled laser diodes) are positioned at
       opposite windows. The images are recorded at 1024x1024 and 30 fps. Thus far, the primary
       purpose of this combined system has been the measurement of the droplet diameter for
       determination of the droplet burning rate.

       A HiBMs camera without the Liquid Crystal Tunable Filter (LCTF) has been used throughout much,
       but not all, of ACME’s operations. In the first round of the CLD Flame experiment, it was used
       without a filter for flame imaging, but this proved to be largely unsuccessful because the camera
       is very insensitive to blue. Afterward, it was equipped with a red filter corresponding to the CIR
       illumination package for the rest of the ACME testing. Unfortunately, that filter is sufficiently
       broad that significant incandescence can pass through the filter limiting its utility for
       determinations of soot volume fraction via light extinction, e.g., through the technique described
       in the following papers:
       D.L. Urban, Z.G. Yuan, P.B. Sunderland, K.C., Lin, Z. Dai, and G.M. Faeth, “Smoke-Point Properties
            of Nonbuoyant Round Laminar Jet Diffusion Flames,” Proceedings of the Twenty-Eighth
            Symposium (International) on Combustion 28 (2000) 1965-1972.
       F.J. Diez, C. Aalburg, P.B. Sunderland, D.L. Urban, Z.-G. Yuan, G.M. Faeth, “Soot properties of
            laminar jet diffusion flames in microgravity,” Combustion and Flame, 156 (2009) 1514-1524.
       P.S. Greenberg and J.C. Ku, “Soot Volume Fraction Imaging,” Applied Optics, 36 (22) (1997) 5514-
            5522.
       Nonetheless, soot volume fraction can also be determined from the color DataCam images.

       During ACME operations, the HiBMs camera is typically archived at 15 fps because of data limits
       for transfer and downlink operations. And because operations are constrained by the data limits,
       image data from this camera are sometimes deleted without downlinking to enable additional
       testing. And in some testing, HiBMs images aren’t even acquired given their lesser value.

       Unfortunately, the HiBMs backlight illumination is significantly obstructed by the bracket for the
       electrode mesh further limiting its utility for the E-FIELD Flames experiment. This was a design
       trade-off necessitated primarily by ACME’s large number of cameras and other optical
       instruments.

       CIR’s Low-Light-Level Ultraviolet (LLL-UV) package is a monochrome camera with an intensifier
       (for 280-700 nm) and a 310-nm bandpass filter (with a 10-nm FWHM) for OH* imaging. Again as
       a result of the signal loss through the filter, binning is employed such that the OH* images are
       recorded at 512x512 and 30 fps.

       During ACME operations, the LLL-UV camera has only been used for the Structure and Response
       of Spherical Diffusion Flames (s-Flame) experiment.

       While not discussed in the preceding section, another intensified camera was used throughout
       ACME testing, although its image data was sometimes deleted or not even acquired to allow the
       performance of additional tests. This was the Cool Flames Investigation (CFI) camera which was
       generally configured with its CH* filter, where an exception is some s-Flame testing with
       particularly dim flames. Due to design trade-offs, its Field of View (FOV) was partially obstructed
       by the arms of the Thin Filament Pyrometry (TFP) fiber array when it was moved inwards to the
       flame. More information about this camera can be found in its characterization report, CIR-RPT-
       4600.

       The ACME chamber insert will also be equipped with non-imaging optical diagnostics including
       both thermopile radiometers and Photomultiplier Tubes (PMTs). There is a crew-exchangeable

       linear array of radiometers of which there are two versions supporting the initial set of
       experiments. The array has a fixed position which is parallel to the chamber axis and centered
       (along the chamber length) relative to the chamber windows and thus most of the other
       instrumentation. One array is equipped with a single wide-view radiometer with two narrow-
       view radiometers that are downstream and two more that are upstream, where all views are
       orthogonal to the chamber axis, as depicted in Figure 4. The narrow-view radiometers are
       intended to support studies of gas-jet flames, where ACME can accommodate shorter burners,
       making the “lower” detectors useful. The other array, also seen in Fig. 4, provides for three wide-
       view measurements of the flame, where one view is masked to nominally block radiant emission
       from the burner. It was specifically designed for the BRE experiment but will be a resource
       available to other ACME experiments. Given that the radiometer array can be exchanged by the
       crew, alternate arrays could be developed.




         Figure 4: Crew-exchangeable radiometer arrays on the ACME chamber insert: (top) standard
       (ACME) array of 5 radiometers, and (bottom) array with three radiometers, designed for the BRE
                experiment, where the lowest detector is masked to avoid view of the burner.

       A third radiometer array was developed for the CFI-G experiment, where it is important to
       understand that only a single radiometer array can be used at a time.

       The narrow-view radiometers in the standard array are only fully sensitive in small spots, but they
       are each sensitive in a much greater Field of View (FOV) than represented in the preceding figure.
       They do not have lenses; their FOV is a result of an aperture where most of their FOV is within the
       penumbra which is only visible to a portion of the sensor. Figures representing the measured FOVs
       of the as-built standard and BRE radiometer arrays are found in section 3.F of this document.

       Unfortunately, the radiometers in the BRE array typically saturated in the first round of BRE
       testing, so the standard array was used in its second round. However, those radiometers similarly
       saturated in many of round 2 tests. In this regard, each of ACME’s BRE and standard radiometers
       have a fixed gain. Meanwhile, each sensor in the radiometer array developed for the CFI-G
       investigation has two fixed gains, i.e., a high gain and low gain.

       For more information about the standard radiometer array used for most ACME testing, see the
       Radiometer Characterization Report (ACME-RPT-011). Meanwhile, you can learn about the other
       radiometer arrays from the BRE Radiometer Characterization Report (ACME-RPT-012) and the
       Cool Flames Radiometer Characterization Report (CIR-RPT-4600).

       An additional wide-view radiometer, that is fixed and not crew exchangeable, is positioned
       downstream of the burner and flame at 30o from the chamber axis. This detector is primarily
       intended to support the spherical flame experiments and will have a quicker response than the
       more sensitive detectors that will used in the linear arrays. Like the radiometers on the two linear
       arrays, it will have a spectral range of nominally 0.2-11 micron and its field of view will be
       established by an appropriately selected aperture.

       The FOV of the off-set radiometer is also shown in section 3.F. Learn more about this sensor from
       the Radiometer Characterization Report (ACME-RPT-011).

       To detect the quenching of dim flames, the chamber insert will be equipped with a set of three
       PMTs. Each PMT will have a wide-angle view, a sight line that is orthogonal to the chamber axis,
       and will be centered (along the chamber length) relative to the chamber windows so that it is
       aligned with most of the other instrumentation. The PMTs’ spectral range will be 230-700 nm,
       i.e., visible and near ultraviolet, but one will have a 310-nm bandpass filter and another will have
       a 430-nm bandpass filter (where both are with a 10-nm FWHM). The pair of detectors will enable
       measurement of OH* and CH* emission, respectively. The third PMT will be equipped with a
       neutral density filter to prevent saturation. Like many other components, the PMT array can be
       removed from the chamber insert by the crew.

       Unlike the radiometers, each PMT had a readily adjustable gain. However, the PMT signals
       sometimes saturated when the gains were set too high. See the PMT Characterization Report
       (ACME-RPT-013) to learn more about the three Photomultiplier Tube (PMTs).

       The chamber insert is also equipped with six far-field thermocouples to measure the spread of
       the thermal field in the spherical flame experiments. They are of limited utility for other
       experiments because they are positioned upstream of the burner tip. Therefore, they will

                                                   11
ACME Engineering Implementation                                                              19 May 2022


       generally not be installed by the crew to reduce the chance of accidental damage. There will be
       two versions of a four-thermocouple rake to allow a choice of near and far measurements. Other
       arrays could be designed to support future experiments, but they cannot extend downstream of
       the burner tip without interfering with other (e.g., optical) measurements.

       The two lone thermocouples, which are both at nominally 200 mm from the center of the spherical
       burner, have been installed throughout ACME testing. The tip of one was seen to be slightly bent
       early in testing, as discussed and depicted in section 3.G of this report.

       Only the short (or far) version of the radiometer rake has been used thus far, where factors include
       the need for crew time to switch between rakes and the heat loss that could result from
       thermocouples in the long (close) rake. The short (far) rake’s thermocouple tips should nominally
       be at 45, 60, 100, and 100 mm from the spherical burner center. For thermocouples at the same
       distance from the burner center, the angle between the rays from the burner center to the two
       thermocouples should be 90 degrees. The bead size of the thermocouples was not measured, but
       it is undoubtedly at least twice the size of the 0.2 mm diameter wire.

       The two thermocouples closest to the burner outlets on each four-thermocouple rake are type B,
       while the others are type K.

       Experiment-unique hardware is also mounted on the chamber insert. For example, to
       accommodate the E-FIELD Flames experiment, the insert is equipped with a high-voltage supply
       and a crew-removable electrode mesh which can be set between +10 and -10 kV. The circular mesh
       is orthogonal to the chamber axis and is positioned (during set up) at either ~35 or ~50 mm
       downstream of the insulated burner. The ion current between the burner and electrode mesh will
       be determined via measurement of the voltage drop across a known resistor and application of
       Ohm’s Law.

       Additional images of the chamber insert can be seen in subsequent sections of this report. The
       hardware specific to E-FIELD Flames is discussed further in section 2.B.

       Additional supporting measurements will be made, where the chamber pressure is a simple
       example. The ACME experiments will use the CIR’s gas chromatograph for pre- and post-test
       analysis of the chamber atmosphere, e.g., for oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide,
       and fuel gases. Acceleration measurements will be provided by the Space Acceleration
       Measurement System (SAMS) [27], which is the primary instrument for such measurements on
       ISS and previously served that role for the space shuttle.

       The CIR’s Gas Chromatograph Instrumentation Package (GCIP) was not used for the CLD Flame or
       E-FIELD Flames investigations and thus far was only sometimes used during BRE, Flame Design,
       and s-Flame testing. Its use requires significant time and as such effectively reduces the number
       of tests that can be conducted. However, estimates of the chamber contents were computed
       assuming that the fuel in a normal flame test (for example) was either fully or not at all oxidized,
       e.g., for unsustained ignition in the latter case.

       The CIR facility was equipped with a 3-axis SAMS sensor head and the acceleration measurements,
       along with relevant documentation, are accessible via the home page for the Principal Investigator

       All ACME experiments will be constant volume (i.e., rather than constant pressure) studies, where
       the chamber’s free volume will be about 90 liters. Burner flows will generally be constrained to
       limit the pressure rise, and tests will typically be 0.5-2 minutes in duration.

       The chamber’s free volume is approximately 83 liters depending on the installed hardware.

       The ACME experiments will be configured, but not conducted, by the ISS crew. Instead, the
       experiments will be commanded from the ground, specifically from the Telescience Support
       Center (TSC) at the NASA Glenn Research Center in Cleveland, Ohio. They can be conducted in a
       nominally automated mode using pre-programmed scripts. However, it will also be possible to
       effectively operate the experiments in a “manual” mode, where changes can be made in response
       to the analog video downlink and the numerical data available in the telemetry stream. For
       example, a flow rate could be adjusted to approach a stability or soot limit. Step and ramp
       changes can be made in the flow rate(s) or electric field potential. The TFP fiber array can be
       translated uniformly through a soot-free flame to get a continuum measurement of the
       temperature field. In summary, ACME’s experiment control will allow for broad flexibility.

       The TSC is now also referred to as the Glenn ISS Payload Operations Center (GIPOC). Thus far, the
       ACME tests have always been conducted in a nominally automated mode.

       The E-FIELD Flames experiment is being conducted with two different burners: a simple gas-jet
       burner and a coflow burner, which can be seen in Figure 4(a). The results discussed in this paper
       are only from the first of the experiment’s tests conducted with the coflow burner. The stainless
       steel fuel tube in the burner has inner and outer diameter of 2.2 and 2.4 mm, respectively. The
       coflow burner’s steel outer tube has an inner diameter of 25 mm, where the annulus outlet is a
       6.4-mm thick Hastelloy X honeycomb with 0.05-mm foil thickness and 0.81-mm cell size. The
       coflow burner’s inner tube protrudes 3.1 mm beyond the surface of the honeycomb outlet. While
       the inner tube is being used exclusively for fuel flow in E-FIELD Flames, valves in the ACME
       chamber insert allow the fuel, oxidizer, and nitrogen to be directed to either tube, making this

       In this study, a high-voltage electric field is generated between the burner and a copper electrode
       mesh located above (i.e., downstream of) the burner outlet, as can be seen in Figure 4(b). The
       circular mesh is energized at up to 10,000 volts with either polarity, where the burner serves as
       the electrical return and the flow of flame-produced ions completes the circuit. The ion current
       between the burner and electrode mesh is measured from the voltage drop across a known resistor
       and application of Ohm’s Law. With and without the copper ring, the electrode mesh is 8.9 and
       8.4 cm in diameter, respectively. The square copper weave has 7.1 wires/cm, a wire diameter of
       0.43 mm, and a 48% open area. The mesh permits gas to pass through while also acting as an
       electrical element. The electrode mesh is mounted 37 mm above the coflow burner tip and is
       centered on and orthogonal to the chamber axis. The ACME chamber insert can also


                                                   16
ACME Engineering Implementation                                                               19 May 2022


       accommodate an alternate electrode position of 52 mm above the coflow burner tip, but the
       closer position is being used to produce the highest field strengths.

       The slow response time of the high-voltage power supplies (recommended in the ACME
       Integrated Science Requirements Document, i.e., ISRD) did not meet the requirements necessary
       to achieve the requested time response performance, and as such inhibited determination of the
       flame’s response time to rapid changes in the electric field strength.

       The negative and positive high-voltage power supplies were to have been coupled, as described
       for example in Appendix C of the ISRD. However, switching between the two high-voltage power
       supplies led to excessive Electromagnetic Interference (EMI) in ground testing so the power
       supplies were completely decoupled, where only one was installed at a time. As such, testing was
       limited to a single polarity at a time in contrast to the experimental plans documented in the
       experiment’s Science Requirements Document (SRD) and the ACME ISRD.

       ACME’s non-imaging measurements are recorded at 125 Hz. The measured test conditions
       include the chamber pressure and temperature, the three gas flow rates, and the electrical
       potential of the electrode mesh. Acceleration measurements are provided by the Space
       Acceleration Measurement System (SAMS) [18], which is the primary instrument for such
       measurements on ISS and previously served that role for the space shuttle.

       The flame behavior is observed optically with a variety of instruments. For most ACME
       experiments, including E-FIELD Flames, the radiant emission is measured with 6 thermopile
       detectors with a spectral range of nominally 0.2-11 microns. Five radiometers are in a crew-
       exchangeable linear array that is mounted parallel to the chamber axis. The other detector is
       downstream and off axis from the burner. Chemiluminescent emission is measured with a set of
       three Photomultiplier Tube (PMT) modules. Their spectral range is 230-700 nm, i.e. in the visible
       and near ultraviolet, but two are equipped with narrow bandpass filters for measurement of OH*
       and CH* emissions, respectively. The third PMT is equipped with a neutral density filter to prevent
       saturation, although the gain values for all three PMTs are independently and readily adjustable.
       Like many of the components, the array of PMT modules can be removed from the chamber insert
       by the crew. In addition to their scientific value, PMT measurements are used operationally to
       detect ignition and automatically deactivate and retract the igniter. The radiometer or PMT
       measurements can similarly be used to detect extinction and automatically end the gas flow and
       test.

       Imaging measurements of the flames complement the optical sensors described above. Color
       imaging is provided by an analog color camera within the chamber and a high-definition (1360 x
       1024), 12-bit color camera mounted outside the chamber on CIR’s optical bench. The latter is
       equipped with a motorized lens with independent control of the zoom, iris, and focus. It has a
       filter barrel with a fused silica window (for color imaging), a blue-green BG7 Schott glass filter,
       and bandpass filters for imaging CH* and HCHO (formaldehyde) chemiluminescence. The digital
       color camera was characterized prior to launch for color-ratio pyrometry and measurement of the
       soot volume fraction, via the techniques of [19, 20]. The camera makes soot pyrometry possible
       as well as Thin Filament Pyrometry (TFP), where the chamber insert is equipped with a translating
       array of silicon-carbide (SiC) fibers. A 14-micron fiber array can be seen at the top of Figure 4(b).
       However, the fibers interfere with the electric field preventing the use of Thin Filament Pyrometry
       in the E-FIELD Flames experiment.

          3. Spherical Burners
              The spherical burners have thus far been used for the CFI-G, Flame Design, and s-Flame
              experiments. In contrast to initial plans, spaceflight burners were only fabricated with a
              6.35-mm porous sphere. It was initially expected that flames which were more spherical
              might be generated with larger diameter porous spheres, but 2.2-s drop testing indicated
              that 6.35-mm diameter spheres were as good or better than the larger sizes in creating
              spherical flames.

              Multiple copies of the spherical burner were launched to the ISS because of concerns that
              they might be damaged and/or might prove to yield flames which aren’t sufficiently
              spherically symmetric.

              The porous spheres were fabricated from sintering 316 stainless steel with 10-micron
              media grade, for which the porosity is approximately 41% and the density is 4.6 g/cm3.
              More specifically, each sphere was formed by sintering together sintered disks, where the
              supply tube should extend to the sphere’s center. Neglecting the interior of the supply
              tube, there is presumably no cavity within the porous sphere.

              Each sphere was furthermore sinter-bonded to its stainless-steel supply tube. As a side
              effect, the tubing lost its temper and became very pliable because of the high
              temperature required for the bonding. Therefore, a close-fitting support tube, with an
              outer diameter of 1.8 mm, was added to the design. It doesn’t quite extend to the porous
              sphere as can be seen in Figure 46. The gap between the support tube and the burner is
              often obvious in flame imaging because the supply tube is more reflective than the
              support tube.

              Each burner was equipped with an extremely fine, sheathed, type-K thermocouple which
              was embedded as much as ~1 mm into the porous sphere. The thermocouple hole was
              0.76 to 1.0 mm deep (and 0.25-0.30 mm in diameter) where the thermocouple was meant
              to be inserted fully, i.e., until reaching the hole’s bottom, but that may not be its exact
              position. The junction is of course not at the extreme end of the sheathed thermocouple,
              but it is very fine and presumably close. If the porous sphere is imagined as a globe with
              the supply tube as the south pole, the thermocouple is inserted in the lower hemisphere,
              between the equator and pole as seen in Figure 46.




                                                 43
ACME Engineering Implementation                                                             19 May 2022




              Figure 46. A spherical burner, prior to launch, showing the positioning of the slightly

              The spherical burners were first used for the Flame Design experiment, where the first
              flame tests were conducted on 25 April 2019. However, after several days of testing it
              was concluded by the investigators that the flames were insufficiently symmetric at high
              burner flow rates to meet the science objectives. At the high flow rates, the flame ceased
              to be spherically symmetric and its center didn’t coincide with the burner’s center. The
              results indicate that the sintered attachment of the sphere to the supply tube was more
              porous than the sintered sphere itself, where the flame was shifted toward the supply
              tube. Therefore, the spherical burner was replaced on 14 June 2019 where the shift was
              from serial number 1002 to 1003. The subsequent burner (s/n 1003) showed similar
              behavior, but to a significantly reduced degree. As a result, some of the early Flame Design
              testing was repeated after the burner swap. This burner (s/n 1003) has thus far been
              used for all subsequent spherical flame testing including the tests for the s-Flame and CFI-
              G experiments.
